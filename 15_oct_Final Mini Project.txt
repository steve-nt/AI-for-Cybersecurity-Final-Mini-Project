Mini Project: An Explainable and Robust Cyber-Defence Model 

AI for Cybersecurity 

Note: the mini project pulls together everything from all modules, on a problem you choose yourselves. You are not 
asked to invent a new method. You are asked to build a working detector, explain it, test whether it survives with 
imperfect data, and report all of that honestly. A negative result, reported clearly, is worth more than a good number 
you cannot defend. 

1. Choosing your problem and dataset 
Your group picks one security problem and one public dataset. 
Some options: 

• UNSW-NB15 ,network intrusion with a different feature set from CICIDS. 
• NSL-KDD ,the classic intrusion benchmark. Small and fast, but dated, say so in your report. 
• CIC-DDoS2019 ,several DDoS attack types, strongly imbalanced. 
• CIC-IoT2023 or Bot-IoT ,traffic from IoT devices. 
• SMS spam / smishing ,text data, so your pipeline will differ from the labs. 
• Your own proposal ,any public, labelled cybersecurity dataset 
These are only suggestions. You are free to use any public, labelled cybersecurity dataset, including the one used by 
the paper your group presented at the seminar. Taking that paper's dataset and rebuilding part of its pipeline 
yourselves is a good way to start, and it means you already know the problem 

2. What you must build 
Parts A to E and Part G are required. Part F is required for grade 5. Make sure your notebook runs from top to bottom 
in under five minutes. Subsample if you need to, and say that you did. 

Part A. The problem and the data 

• State the security problem in two or three sentences: what is being detected, who would use the system, and 
what a false alarm costs them. 
• Load and clean the data. Report how many records and features you have, and the class balance. 
• Split 60 / 20 / 20 into training, validation and test, stratified. 
• Report the trivial baseline: the accuracy of always predicting the majority class, and its recall. Every later score 
is compared against this. 
Part B. Supervised models 

• Train at least one glass box (decision tree or logistic regression) and one black box (random forest or gradient 
boosting). 
• Report macro-F1, recall, PR-AUC and FAR on the test set, next to the trivial baseline. 
• Choose all settings on the validation set. The test set is touched once, at the end. 
Part C. Learning with fewer labels 

• Keep only 5 to 10 per cent of the training labels. Train your black box on those alone. This is your lower line. 
• Then try either semi-supervised pseudo-labelling or a self-supervised pretext task followed by a classifier 
trained on the few labels. 
• Say plainly whether the unlabelled data helped.. A clear negative result, properly explained, scores as well as a 
positive one. 
Part D. Explanation 

• Global: SHAP on your black box. Which features drive it, and do they make sense to a security person? 
• Local: explain three cases with SHAP and with LIME ,a confident correct detection, a confident correct negative, 
and a mistake. 
• Fidelity: run a deletion test. Replace the top SHAP features with training medians and compare the drop with 
that from the same number of random features. 

• Stability: run LIME several times with different seeds and report how often the top three features stay the 
same. 
Part E. Robustness 

At least one of the following, reported with numbers at several levels: 

• Noise ,add Gaussian noise scaled to each feature's training standard deviation. 
• Missing features ,replace a fraction of each record's values with the training median. 
• Evasion ,a constrained attack. State which features an attacker could realistically change and in which direction, 
and keep to it. 
• Say what broke first, and at what level you would stop trusting the model. 
Part F. A knowledge-based layer 

• Take the two most important features from Part D and build a BRB: three referential values each, nine rules. 
• Compare it with a tree and a forest that see only those same two features, so the comparison is fair. 
• Show the belief output for one case, including the Unknown part. 
• Say what the BRB gives you that the forest does not, and what it costs you. 
Part G. Adaptability 

Everything above tests your model on the same kind of data it was trained on. But attacks change. A new variant 
appears that nobody has seen before, and a model that scored well last month quietly starts missing things. This part 
measures that, on your own single dataset. You do not need any extra data. 

Step 1. Hide one attack from the model. Pick one attack type in your data, for example one DDoS variant, one 
malware family, or one category of fraud. Remove every row of that type from your training set only. Keep them in 
the test set. Now train your black box again on this reduced training set. Your model has never seen this attack. 

Step 2. Measure what it costs. On the test set, report two numbers: overall recall, and recall on the hidden attack 
type by itself. The second number is usually very low. That is the point: the model did not get worse at what it knew, 
the world moved and the model stayed still. 

Step 3. Adapt. Now imagine an analyst has just labelled a small number of examples of the new attack, say 50 to 100 
rows. Add only those to your training set, retrain, and measure the same two numbers again. Report the gain: recall 
on the new attack before adapting, and after. 

Step 4. Say what you would do. In one short paragraph: was a small number of labels enough? How would you notice 

this problem in a real system, where nobody tells you a new attack has appeared? 
If your dataset has only attack and normal labels, with no attack types, use any column that marks a period, a 
source or a site instead. Train on some values of that column and test on the rest. If there is nothing like that either, 
sort the rows by time or by index, train on the first 70 per cent and test on the last 30 per cent. Say in your report 
which of these you did and why.
